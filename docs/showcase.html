Ma no Iro
The colour of the interval.

A two-flavor palette for code and terminals, and a short guide to how colour works: in light, in the eye, in the models we draw it with, and in the traditions that named it.

Scroll, or use the arrow keys.

Every colour comes with the colour it takes away.

Fire, stars and screens make colour by giving light off. Almost everything else you see makes it by taking light away: a leaf, the sky, water and ink each keep part of the light that reaches them and let the rest through to your eye. What reaches you leans toward the complement of what was taken, the colour on the far side of white.

Ma no Iro is built from four of those exchanges, and from the idea underneath them: a thing can be known by what it does not keep. Japanese has a word for form defined by absence, for the pause in music or the empty space in a room. It is 間, ma, the interval.

Strictly speaking. A real object removes a range of wavelengths, not a single colour, so what is left only leans toward the exact complement. The pairs in Ma no Iro are exact because they are solved in a colour model, as the rest of this page shows.

A leaf takes purple from sunlight, and gives back green.

Chlorophyll absorbs strongly at both ends of the visible spectrum, the blue and the red, and much less in the middle. Light from both ends at once is what we see as purple, so what the leaf takes, seen together, is purple, and what it gives back looks green. Some grow lights skip the middle entirely, and glow purple, for the same reason.

Anchor: 555 nm. This pair is anchored not in the leaf but in the eye: 555 nm is where human vision is most sensitive in daylight, as the eye scene shows.

Strictly speaking. A leaf absorbs most of the green light too; it looks green because green is what it absorbs least. It also reflects a great deal of infrared, which we cannot see.

The sky takes blue out of sunlight, and leaves the sun yellower.

Air molecules scatter short wavelengths far more than long ones, in proportion to one over the wavelength to the fourth power. The scattered blue reaches you from every direction and fills the sky. The direct sunlight that gets through has lost some of its blue and looks a little yellower, and much more so at sunset, when it passes through more air.

Anchor: the λ⁻⁴ curve. The same curve is the limit a glowing hot body approaches as its temperature rises without end, so the blue of the sky and the blue of infinite heat are one colour.

Strictly speaking. Sunlight is not an even spectrum to begin with, and scattering is even stronger in violet; the sky looks blue rather than violet partly because the sun gives off less violet and our eyes are less sensitive to it.

Water takes red, so it looks cyan.

The bonds in a water molecule vibrate, and a few of those vibrations absorb red light. The effect is invisible in a glass but adds up over metres, so deep clear water and glacier ice show what is left: cyan and blue. Water is one of very few everyday things whose colour comes from its molecules vibrating rather than from its electrons.

Anchor: 700 nm, the end of red. In the CIE standard observer, every longer visible wavelength has exactly the same chromaticity, so this is the reddest red there is. Cyan is its complement.

Strictly speaking. The colour of a real lake or sea also depends on what is dissolved and floating in it, and on the sky it reflects.

Ink takes everything. Paper gives it all back.

Sumi ink is made of soot: fine carbon that absorbs almost every visible wavelength. Paper, like snow, is a tangle of fibres that scatters every wavelength back about equally. So black and white are not a colour and its opposite but the amount of light itself, none of it and all of it.

In Ma no Iro they are the ground: pure grey from black to white, so no colour is ever spent on the background.

Strictly speaking. No ink is perfectly black and no paper perfectly white; both return a little light, and paper often a little more blue than red.

Paper and ink: all of the light, and almost none of it.schematic

The eye turns a spectrum into three numbers.

Your retina has three kinds of cone, long, medium and short, most sensitive near 570, 545 and 445 nm. Every colour you see is how strongly light excites those three. Two quite different spectra can excite them equally and look identical, which is why a screen can show you a leaf with only three kinds of light.

In daylight, your sense of brightness peaks at 555 nm, in the green: Ma no Iro’s first anchor. And purple exists only here. No single wavelength excites the long and short cones while sparing the middle one, so purple is a colour the eye makes, not one the rainbow has.

Behind the cones, the visual system compares them. One comparison runs roughly between green and magenta, the other roughly between violet and yellow, which is part of why those colours feel like opposites.

Strictly speaking. The comparisons measured in the retina do not line up exactly with the opposites people name, red against green and blue against yellow. Vision science has not fully reconciled the two.

Eight colours, four pairs, one grey.

A screen makes every colour from three lights, red, green and blue, so the colours it can show fill a cube. The eight corners are the eight colours a terminal names. Opposite corners are complements, pairs that add up to white, and the four lines joining them all cross at the grey at the centre. Look straight down from white to black and the cube becomes a hexagon: the colour wheel, with grey at its heart.

That grey is where Ma no Iro takes its name. Every pair leaves it in opposite directions, and each colour is defined by the one it lacks.

Strictly speaking. The cube is a model of a screen, not of light or of the eye. Its complements are exact for a screen’s three lights; painters mixing pigments, and perception itself, pair colours a little differently.

Older traditions drew similar lines.

正色 間色

Classical Chinese colour theory named five cardinal colours, black, white, red, yellow and 青 (blue-green), and a set of intermediary colours mixed between them. In one common list, green, purple and jade (綠, 紫, 碧) are among those intermediaries: three of the terminal’s colours, in the interval.

陰陽

Yin and yang are opposites that each hold a seed of the other. Ma no Iro borrows the image for its two flavors: a night that keeps a dot of green, and a day that keeps a dot of purple.

間

In Japanese aesthetics, ma is the interval that gives a form its shape: the gap between stones in a garden, the pause between notes. It is the same character that names the intermediary colours, and it is the idea that a colour can be known by what it lacks.

Strictly speaking. Classical colour words do not map one to one onto modern hues: 青 covers both blue and green, and lists of intermediary colours differ between sources. These are correspondences, not proofs, and nothing in the palette depends on them.

Three anchors. Every hue is solved from them.

Colorimetry maps every colour a person can see onto one chart, the CIE 1931 chromaticity diagram. Pure wavelengths run around its curved edge; the purples close the bottom with a straight line, because no single wavelength makes them. White sits inside.

Each pair in Ma no Iro has one anchor, and its partner lies on the straight line through white, on the far side. Two anchors are limits, the end of red and the curve of the sky, and the third is the eye. Move the eye’s anchor and every purple moves with it.

Strictly speaking. The diagram shows chromaticity and ignores brightness. Hues in the palette are solved; lightness and chroma are design choices, set so that text stays readable.

Night and day, each keeping a dot of the other.

In the formula screens use for brightness, green contributes about 72% of white, and red and blue together about 28%. So green belongs to the day flavor and purple to the night. Like a yin-yang, each keeps a dot of the other half, and spends it only on what you are looking at: the cursor, the current match, the focused control.

Strictly speaking. The pairing is a design choice grounded in that brightness split, not a law. The seed is placed where it is most visible on each ground: bright green on black, deep purple on white.

Code, written in the leaf, the sky and the water.

Keywords are purple and functions are green. Strings are the sky’s blue and types are the water’s cyan; numbers and escapes are a pale purple. Red and yellow never colour code: they are kept for errors, deletions, warnings and search. Selections are grey steps of the ground, so nothing under them changes colour, and there is one green in code, so an added line is the only green background you will see.

Strictly speaking. The scenes explain where each colour comes from, not what it means in code; which token gets which colour is a design choice, made so that the most frequent tokens carry the palette’s own pair.
